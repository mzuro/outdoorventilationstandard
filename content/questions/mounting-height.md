At a 24-inch mounting height, the plume's centerline velocity is about **317 fpm**; at 30 inches, about **295 fpm**; at 36 inches, about **277 fpm**. A wall hood needs to sustain at least 100 fpm of capture velocity at the hood face; this analysis uses 150 fpm as the island margin threshold — so all three heights clear the threshold in still air, but the margin above the island threshold shrinks from roughly 2.1x at 24 inches to about 1.8x at 36 inches.

## Two things trade off with height

Mounting lower keeps velocity higher and gives wind less time to deflect the plume before it reaches the hood — both favor capture. But mounting lower also means the plume hasn't spread as wide yet, so the hood's overhang has to be sized to a narrower plume and there's less vertical clearance for a tall stockpot or a flare-up. Mounting higher gains headroom and lets the plume widen further before capture — which the hood's overhang has to accommodate — while giving up velocity margin and giving wind more time to act.

## Why the margin, not just the minimum, matters

A hood sized to just clear 150 fpm at 36 inches has far less room for error than one at 24 inches — a gusty day, a hotter-than-usual load, or a slightly undersized blower can push effective velocity below threshold with no more room to give. The instrument above traces centerline velocity continuously from grill surface to full rise; the two dashed reference lines mark the wall and island thresholds so the actual margin at any height is visible directly, not just implied by a single number. The full [Velocity Decay Curves](/tools/velocity-decay-curves/) tool shows this same trace on its own page alongside the source-type comparisons.

## Practical guidance

30 inches is the standard reference height in this analysis and a reasonable default for most installs — it holds velocity comfortably above threshold while giving usable clearance for tall cookware. Sites with above-average wind exposure should mount toward the lower end of the practical range rather than the higher end, since velocity margin and deflection time both work against a high mount in wind. See the wind and overhang questions for how those two effects compound at a given height.
