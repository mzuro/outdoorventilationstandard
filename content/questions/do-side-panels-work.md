On a 48-inch island hood at a 30-inch mounting height in an 8 mph side wind, capture goes from about **20% with no side panels** to about **70% with panels on both sides**; at 5 mph, from 51% to 80%. That is a bigger swing than any change of exhaust rate can produce at those wind speeds, and it's achievable without touching the blower at all.

## Why panels are so effective

A side panel is really a wind guard: it doesn't block the plume — it blocks the wind that's deflecting it. Two side panels extending two-thirds of the way from the hood down to the cooking surface carry a wind-reduction coefficient of 0.60 against a lateral wind in RB-009's tables, so the plume experiences **40% of the ambient speed** — 3.2 mph in an 8 mph breeze (RB-009 Table 3.1a, f = 0.67). Deflection is proportional to wind speed (RB-006 §3.1), so the centerline's 18.8-inch excursion at 8 mph shrinks to about 7.5 inches, back inside the hood's 12-inch overhang. The instrument above is set to the island scenario with no panels by default — toggle the panel control to see the same 8 mph breeze produce a very different result. The full [Side Panel Effectiveness](/tools/side-panel-effectiveness/) tool compares the with/without capture gap across the whole wind range.

## This is the cheapest fix for a windy site

Unlike width and overhang — which are design-locked at installation (see [what size hood your grill needs](/questions/what-size-hood-for-my-grill/)) — side panels can usually be added after the fact. For a site that's already built and underperforming in wind, panels are very often the highest-leverage change available — more effective than a bigger blower, and far less disruptive than remounting the hood lower or rebuilding the structure to add a back wall. RB-006's combined-mitigation table makes the same point for the papers' recommended 57-inch hood: side panels alone recover 15–20 points of capture at 5 mph and are the most effective single intervention (RB-006 §3.10).

## Panels and wind direction

Two side panels shelter against wind blowing across the hood face. Against wind from the front or rear the same panels cut the wind by only about 15% (RB-009 Table 3.1a), so a site whose prevailing wind comes from behind the grill needs a rear panel — RB-009's three-sided configuration (RB-009 Table 3.1b) — or a wall, which does the job by itself (RB-006 §3.9.2; see [island vs. wall](/questions/island-vs-wall-hood/)). A single panel is not modeled here: no paper tabulates it, and RB-009 warns that a lone windward panel sets up a recirculation that can carry plume gas out the open side (RB-009 §3.5). Panels also need depth to work — at least half the mounting height, with diminishing returns beyond three-quarters (RB-009 §3.4).

## The airflow dividend

Panels also lower the exhaust rate an exposed site needs. RB-008's multiplier for an exposed site drops from 5.75 without panels to 4.14 with them — a 28% reduction — which for a 60,000 BTU grill at 30 inches means 1,004 CFM instead of 1,394 (RB-008 §2.2, §3.3). See [what CFM you need](/questions/what-cfm-do-i-need/).

## Bottom line

If a hood is underperforming specifically in wind — smoke escaping on breezy days but not on calm ones — side panels on the windward axis are the first thing to try, not a bigger fan.
