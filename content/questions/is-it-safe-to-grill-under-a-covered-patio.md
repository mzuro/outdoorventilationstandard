Grilling under a covered patio is conditionally workable, not automatically safe. The condition doing the work in that sentence is the roof: outdoors under open sky, smoke and combustion gases that escape the hood rise and dilute to imperceptible levels within a few meters (RB-004 §2.1). A roof removes that escape path. Whatever your hood does not capture no longer disperses — it collects.

## What a roof actually changes

The research program's transport analysis assumes open sky: escaped plume material travels "until it deposits on the first surface it encounters," and outdoors there are no ceiling surfaces to intercept it (RB-011 §1). Put a roof over the grill and the first surface is the underside of that roof. Heat, smoke, and grease aerosol that would have left vertically now accumulate overhead and around anyone standing there. None of the six enclosure mechanisms that make *indoor* kitchens work — walls guiding air back to the exhaust, code-required makeup air, a finite room volume the exhaust continuously turns over (RB-004 §2.1) — exist on a patio. A covered patio is not a mild version of outdoors; it is a partial enclosure with none of an enclosure's engineered protections. The RB papers model open sky and fully enclosed rooms; they do not model the in-between, and this page does not pretend otherwise.

## Combustion gases, including carbon monoxide

Burning gas, charcoal, wood, or pellets produces combustion byproducts, and carbon monoxide is among them — that is basic combustion chemistry, not a finding of this research program. Be clear about what the program does and does not cover: the RB papers characterize how the plume moves and where it goes; they do not measure gas composition, CO concentration, or exposure, and nothing on this page is a CO exposure assessment. What the physics does say is that the open-air dilution that keeps byproduct concentrations negligible around an open grill (RB-004 §2.1) is precisely the mechanism a roof takes away. The more enclosed the space — roof plus walls, screens, or drop-down shades — the less that safety margin exists. Your appliance manual's placement restrictions and your local code authority govern whether a specific covered installation is permitted; check both before building, not after.

## Fire clearances are the hard constraint

Two separate fire mechanisms documented in the papers get worse under a roof:

**Grease on overhead surfaces.** The dominant outdoor grease-fire scenario is not spontaneous ignition — it is a flare-up or ember reaching a surface that has quietly accumulated grease over many cooking sessions (RB-011 §3.6.1). Under a roof, the structure itself becomes a deposition surface. RB-011 (§3.6.3) notes the NFPA-recommended minimum of 10 feet between grills and combustible structures; a covered patio puts combustible structure permanently inside that radius, which is exactly why capture, cleaning, and clearances have to carry the load instead of distance.

**Heat above the hood.** Hood surfaces run hot enough to matter: at a 30-inch mount, most sources push peak hood-surface temperatures to roughly 95–178&nbsp;°C — above the 71&nbsp;°C threshold the codes cited in RB-012 use for clearance to combustibles (RB-012 §3.7). The hood itself will not ignite anything, but wood framing directly above it needs clearance, heat shielding, or non-combustible construction (RB-012 §3.7). A roof means there *is* something above the hood; plan for it explicitly.

## What a hood does — and what it cannot do

A properly sized hood intercepts the plume at the source, before the roof turns escape into accumulation. That is genuinely the right tool for a covered outdoor kitchen, and a wind-sheltered site is where hoods perform best — see [Can I use an outdoor range hood under a covered patio?](/questions/hood-under-covered-patio/) for the sizing details. But a hood is a capture device, not a certification: it does not make a covered patio "outdoors," it cannot capture 100% of the plume, and the standards that would define acceptable performance for this situation do not yet exist — the existing indoor codes simply do not address outdoor cooking ventilation (RB-010 §3.1&ndash;3.2; RB-004 §4.6). Anyone who tells you a hood *makes* a covered patio safe is selling certainty the physics does not offer.

## The honest checklist

Before grilling under any roof: confirm your appliance's manual permits the placement; ask your local building or fire code authority what applies; keep combustible construction clear of the hood per the clearance principles in RB-012 §3.7 (see [how much clearance an outdoor hood needs](/questions/hood-clearance-to-combustibles/)); size the hood for capture rather than for looks (see [what CFM you need](/questions/what-cfm-do-i-need/)); and clean grease from the hood, filters, and surrounding surfaces on a schedule, because under a roof the deposits stay where they land (RB-011 §3.6). If the space is enclosed enough that it feels like a room, treat it as a room — which means treating this page as no longer applicable and your code authority as the only voice that counts.
