Hood width is a plume question wearing a grill costume. The cooking plume is already about **28 inches** across at the grate and widens as it rises — roughly **34.6 inches** across by a 30-inch mounting height and **38.6 inches** by 48 inches. The hood has to span that widened plume, plus a margin for wind, on the day it's installed; width is design-locked, and no blower upgrade can add inches later.

## Start from the plume, not the grill

Grill width matters only because it anchors where the plume starts. Once the plume leaves the surface it grows by entrainment on every side, so two grills of different widths under the same mounting height need hoods sized to nearly the same *plume*, offset by their different starting widths. The instrument above is set to a 48-inch wall hood on side A against the same width on an island on side B — move the wind control and watch which side gives way first. This page owns the side-to-side question; front-to-back coverage is its own decision, covered in [how far the hood should extend past the grill](/questions/hood-depth-and-overhang/).

## Where the "+6 inches per side" rule comes from — and when it fails

The ubiquitous retail rule — hood 6 inches wider than the grill on each side — is the *indoor* ASHRAE 154 island-canopy minimum carried outdoors unchanged (RB-010 Gap S-5). Run the geometry on a 36-inch grill: the rule yields a 48-inch hood, and against a 34.6-inch-wide plume at a 30-inch mount that leaves about 6.7 inches of margin per side in still air — workable, which is why the rule survives. But a 5 mph breeze deflects the plume centerline by about 13 inches at that height in this model (the papers put it at 12 inches for a medium gas grill — RB-006 Table 3.2b), which is twice the margin the rule left. Consumer hoods built to 6-12 inches of overhang per side sit well short of the 15-26 inches per side the outdoor analysis calls for (RB-010 Gap S-5).

## What the papers recommend

RB-002's engineering tables size hood width from the plume capture diameter times a 1.70 outdoor margin factor. At a 30-inch mount that works out to 51 inches over a small gas grill, 57 inches over a medium, 62 inches over a large, and up to 71 inches over a large wood-fired source (RB-002 Table 3.7) — roughly twice the cooking surface width (RB-002 §3.7). In overhang terms: 15-20 inches per side at 30 inches for all source types, so a 24-inch cooking surface points to a 54-64 inch hood and a 36-inch surface to a 66-76 inch hood (RB-005 §3.1). Hood widths are commonly manufactured in 6-inch increments, so in practice you round the derived requirement up to the next available width; this program does not test or recommend products, so which manufacturer's width you round to is not a question it answers.

## Once the plume is covered, width stops paying

Width is a threshold variable, not a linear one. In the capture model at a steady 5 mph, a 42-inch island hood reads about 78% capture and a 72-inch island hood over the same grill about 79% — one point for thirty inches of steel. What actually separates outcomes at fixed width is mount and wind: the same 48-inch hood at 5 mph models at 99% on a wall and 79% on an island, and at 10 mph the island collapses to about 24% while the wall holds near 87%. Buy enough width to clear the plume with margin, then spend the next dollar on [mount choice](/questions/island-vs-wall-hood/), [wind mitigation](/questions/do-side-panels-work/), and [CFM](/questions/what-cfm-do-i-need/), in that order.

## Practical takeaway

Take your mounting height, size the hood to the plume's width at that height plus a wind margin — not to the grill plus 6 — and round up to the next manufactured width. For a typical 36-inch grill at a 30-inch mount, the physics-derived answer lands well above the retail rule's 48 inches; the exact figure depends on your source type and exposure, and the tables in RB-002 carry all of them.
