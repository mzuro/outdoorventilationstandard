The hood has to span the plume where it meets the hood face, not the grill below it. Over a medium gas grill the capture diameter — the 98% flux contour plus the source width — is about **27 inches** at the grate and widens as it rises: roughly **41 inches** by a 30-inch mounting height and **50 inches** by 48 inches (RB-002 Table 3.3a). The papers then apply a base margin of 1.38 for turbulent intermittency and puffing and publish the result directly: a **57-inch** hood at 30 inches over this grill (RB-002 §3.6, Table 3.7). The design consequence is that the hood must cover the widened plume plus a margin for wind on the day it's installed — width is design-locked, and no blower upgrade can add inches later.

## Start from the plume, not the grill

Grill width matters only because it anchors where the plume starts. Once the plume leaves the surface it grows by entrainment on every side — 0.48 inch of diameter per inch of rise (RB-002 App. A.3) — so two grills of different widths under the same mounting height need hoods sized to nearly the same *plume*, offset by their different starting widths. The instrument above is set to a 48-inch wall hood on side A against the same width on an island on side B; with the wind blowing from behind the grill, watch which side gives way first. This page owns the side-to-side question; front-to-back coverage is its own decision, covered in [how far the hood should extend past the grill](/questions/hood-depth-and-overhang/).

## Where the "+6 inches per side" rule comes from — and when it fails

The ubiquitous retail rule — hood 6 inches wider than the grill on each side — matches the *indoor* ASHRAE 154 island-canopy minimum, and RB-010 observes that consumer outdoor hoods are built with overhang comparable to indoor practice, 6 to 12 inches per side (RB-010 Gap S-5). Run the geometry on a 36-inch grill, which puts it in the papers' high-output gas class (RB-001 Table 3.1): the rule yields a 48-inch hood, and the capture diameter at a 30-inch mount is 48 inches — the hood equals the plume with no margin at all, exactly the minimum overhang RB-005 tabulates for this class and height, which "provides no margin for turbulence, wind, or instantaneous plume fluctuation" (RB-005 §3.1, Table 3.1a). And wind spends whatever margin exists immediately: a 5 mph breeze deflects this grill's plume centerline about 9.5 inches at that height (RB-006 Table 3.2d prints 10). The papers' recommended width for the high-output class at 30 inches is 67 inches — about 15 inches of overhang per side (RB-002 Table 3.7). Consumer hoods built to 6–12 inches of overhang sit well short of the 11–26 inches per side the outdoor analysis calls for across the standard height range (RB-005 §3.1; RB-010 Gap S-5); the broader story of why indoor conventions travel badly is covered in [can I use an indoor range hood outside](/questions/can-i-use-an-indoor-range-hood-outside/).

## What the papers recommend

RB-002's engineering tables apply the base outdoor width-sizing margin (K ≈ 1.38, scaling toward 1.70 for wind-exposed sites per RB-002 §3.5) to the capture diameter and publish the recommended hood widths directly. At a 30-inch mount the tables give 51 inches over a small gas grill, 57 over a medium, 62 over a large and 67 over a high-output one (RB-002 Table 3.7) — roughly twice the cooking surface width (RB-002 §3.7). In overhang terms: 15–20 inches per side at 30 inches for all source types, so a 24-inch cooking surface points to a 54–64 inch hood and a 36-inch surface to a 66–76 inch hood (RB-005 §3.1). Hood widths are commonly manufactured in 6-inch increments, so in practice you round the derived requirement up to the next available width; this program does not test or recommend products, so which manufacturer's width you round to is not a question it answers.

## What an undersized hood costs

The 48-inch preset above is 84% of the 57-inch recommendation. RB-008 rates that band *marginal* — "80–85% at best" in still air — and says to upgrade the width rather than the airflow, because below about 80% of the recommended width no exhaust rate can recover plume that overflows the hood (RB-008 Table 3.10, §3.10). The capture model agrees: in still air the 48-inch hood reads about 87%; in a 5 mph side wind it reads about 51%, against 72% for the 57-inch hood and 94% for a 72-inch one over the same grill. Get the width right first, then turn to [mount choice](/questions/island-vs-wall-hood/), [wind mitigation](/questions/do-side-panels-work/), and [CFM](/questions/what-cfm-do-i-need/), in that order — and note that a wider hood does not raise the CFM requirement (RB-008 §3.4.3).

## Practical takeaway

Take your mounting height, size the hood to the plume's width at that height plus a wind margin — not to the grill plus 6 — and round up to the next manufactured width. For a typical 36-inch grill at a 30-inch mount, the physics-derived answer lands well above the retail rule's 48 inches; the exact figure depends on your source type and exposure, and the tables in RB-002 carry all of them.
