The duct question outdoors is not the indoor question in new clothes — it is a routing question: does the hood's discharge reach open air? Under open sky, escaped or discharged cooking effluent is diluted to imperceptible levels within a few meters through open-boundary dispersion (RB-004 §2.1), so an installation with nothing above the outlet can release exhaust directly at the hood and let the atmosphere finish the job. Put a roof in the way and the answer flips: the covered volume needs the exhaust taken *out* of it, and that is exactly what a duct is for.

## Why "ductless" is an indoor idea

A recirculating hood filters the airstream and returns it to the room. That premise leans on the one thing an enclosed kitchen has and a patio doesn't: a finite air volume the system passes repeatedly through its filters — indoors, a typical kitchen's air turns over through the exhaust every few minutes, so even imperfect single-pass cleaning converges (RB-004 §2.1). Outdoors there is no room to converge on. And single-pass filtration is genuinely imperfect: baffle grease filters capture 85-98% of droplets above 20 micrometers — the coarse end that carries most of the grease mass — but only 5-15% of particles under 1 micrometer and 20-40% of the 1-5 micrometer range (RB-011 §3.1) — while combustion gases pass through a grease filter entirely, which is a matter of what a particle filter is, not a finding of this program. A recirculating hood outdoors would therefore capture the plume only to re-release its fine fraction and all of its gases at head height, subtracting the one advantage open air offers: that an *exhausted* plume leaves.

## Under open sky, the atmosphere is the duct

The research program's transport analysis treats the open atmosphere as the sink: fine aerosol that passes the filters is released at the duct termination and disperses rapidly from the elevated exhaust point (RB-011 §3.1), and outdoors there is no ceiling to intercept a rising discharge — it rises and dilutes irreversibly (RB-002 §2.8). That is why open outdoor installations commonly run the blower at the hood with a short or no duct run: the discharge is already where it needs to be. The performance questions that actually decide whether such a hood works are [width](/questions/what-size-hood-for-my-grill/), [CFM](/questions/what-cfm-do-i-need/), and [wind exposure](/questions/does-wind-affect-my-hood/) — not duct length.

## Under a roof, the duct is the point

Put structure above the hood and the physics of the covered-patio problem applies to the *discharge*, not just the plume: escaped material travels until it deposits on the first surface it encounters, and under a roof the first surface is the roof (RB-011 §1). A hood that vents beneath the same roof it stands under concentrates heat, moisture, and the filter-passing fine fraction at the ceiling — the papers model open sky and enclosed rooms, not this in-between, but both models agree on the mechanism being lost: there is no open boundary until the exhaust is outside the covered volume. So a roofed outdoor kitchen needs a ducted path out — through the roof or to an open side — and the covered situation raises its own, larger questions ([can I use an indoor range hood outside](/questions/can-i-use-an-indoor-range-hood-outside/) covers why indoor assumptions do not transfer).

{{< note label="Scope" >}}
This page answers *whether* a duct is needed, not how to size one. Duct diameter, run length, elbows, static pressure, and blower curves are not covered by the current research papers — RB-008 names blower and duct sizing for outdoor installations as downstream work not yet published. The papers do note the scale of the problem: outdoor exhaust rates of 727-892 CFM imply 10-12 inch round duct where an indoor equivalent runs 6-8 inch (RB-004 §3.9). For the exhaust rate itself, use the [CFM calculator](/tools/cfm-calculator/).

## The bottom line

Judge the installation by where its exhaust ends up. Open sky above the outlet: the atmosphere is the destination, and a duct is plumbing, not performance. A roof above the outlet: duct out of the covered volume or the hood is rearranging smoke rather than removing it. And recirculating designs answer an indoor question that outdoor installations never asked.
