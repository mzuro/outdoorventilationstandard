In the open, often **no** — under open sky, escaped cooking effluent is diluted to imperceptible levels within a few meters through open-boundary dispersion (RB-004 §2.1), and the same open-boundary physics acts on a hood's discharge regardless of why the material left the appliance, so an installation with nothing above the outlet can release exhaust directly at the hood and let the atmosphere finish the job. The real question is routing: does the hood's discharge reach open air? Put a roof in the way and the answer flips: the covered volume needs the exhaust taken *out* of it, and that is exactly what a duct is for.

## Why "ductless" is an indoor idea

A recirculating hood filters the airstream and returns it to the room. That premise leans on the one thing an enclosed kitchen has and a patio doesn't: a finite air volume the system passes repeatedly through its filters — indoors, a typical kitchen's air turns over through the exhaust every few minutes, so even imperfect single-pass cleaning converges (RB-004 §2.1). Outdoors there is no room to converge on. And single-pass filtration is genuinely imperfect: baffle grease filters capture 70-98% of the coarse fraction above 10 micrometers — which carries the majority of the grease mass — but only 5-15% of particles under 1 micrometer and 20-40% of the 1-5 micrometer range (RB-011 §3.1) — while combustion gases pass through a grease filter entirely, which is a matter of what a particle filter is, not a finding of this program. A recirculating hood outdoors would therefore capture the plume only to re-release its fine fraction and all of its gases at head height, subtracting the one advantage open air offers: that an *exhausted* plume leaves.

## Under open sky, the atmosphere is the duct

The research program's transport analysis treats the open atmosphere as the sink: fine aerosol that passes the filters is released at the duct termination and disperses rapidly from the elevated exhaust point (RB-011 §3.1), and outdoors there is no ceiling to intercept a rising discharge — it rises and dilutes irreversibly (RB-002 §2.8). That is why an open outdoor installation can discharge at the hood with a short or no duct run: the discharge is already where it needs to be. The performance questions that actually decide whether such a hood works are [width](/questions/what-size-hood-for-my-grill/), [CFM](/questions/what-cfm-do-i-need/), and [wind exposure](/questions/does-wind-affect-my-hood/) — not duct length.

## Under a roof, the duct is the point

Put structure above the hood and the physics of the covered-patio problem applies to the *discharge*, not just the plume: escaped material travels until it deposits on the first surface it encounters, and under a roof the first surface is the roof (RB-011 §1). A hood that vents beneath the same roof it stands under concentrates heat, moisture, and the filter-passing fine fraction at the ceiling. The papers do treat covered patios — as a wind-exposure class with its own CFM penalty (RB-003 §3.9; RB-004 §3.8) — but they do not model discharge accumulating beneath a roof; what their open-sky and enclosed-room regimes both make clear is the mechanism being lost: there is no open boundary until the exhaust is outside the covered volume. So a roofed outdoor kitchen needs a ducted path out — through the roof or to an open side — and the covered situation raises its own, larger questions ([can I use an indoor range hood outside](/questions/can-i-use-an-indoor-range-hood-outside/) covers why indoor assumptions do not transfer, and the [Indoor vs. Outdoor Ventilation Comparison](/tools/indoor-vs-outdoor-comparison/) tool maps the mechanisms side by side).

{{< note label="Scope" >}}
This page answers *whether* a duct is needed, not how to size one. Duct diameter, run length, elbows, static pressure, and blower curves are not covered by the current research papers — blower and duct sizing for outdoor installations is listed as a planned downstream topic in RB-008's front matter, not yet published. The papers do note the scale of the problem for their reference case, a 60,000 BTU large gas grill at a 30-inch mount: outdoor exhaust rates of 727-892 CFM imply 10-12 inch round duct where the indoor equivalent runs 6-8 inch (RB-004 §3.9). For the exhaust rate itself, use the [CFM calculator](/tools/cfm-calculator/).

## The bottom line

Judge the installation by where its exhaust ends up. Open sky above the outlet: the atmosphere is the destination, and a duct is plumbing, not performance. A roof above the outlet: duct out of the covered volume or the hood is rearranging smoke rather than removing it. And recirculating designs answer an indoor question that outdoor installations never asked.
