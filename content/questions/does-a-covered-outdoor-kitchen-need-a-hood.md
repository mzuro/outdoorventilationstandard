More than an open one does. An uncovered grill gets a powerful ventilation service for free: the atmosphere. Escaped smoke and combustion byproducts rise and dilute to imperceptible levels within a few meters of the cooking surface — an effectively infinite dilution volume (RB-004 §2.1). That free service is why grilling in the open without any hood is usually tolerable. A roof cancels it. Once there is structure overhead, everything the hood fails to capture stops leaving and starts collecting.

## Open air was doing more work than it looked like

The research program's indoor-versus-outdoor comparison identifies six mechanisms an enclosed kitchen uses to assist capture — walls that guide air back to the exhaust, a ceiling jet that recirculates escaped smoke, room pressurization, controlled makeup air, still air, and a finite room volume the exhaust keeps turning over (RB-004 §2.1). Open-air installations have none of them, but they get unlimited dilution in exchange: escape is permanent, and permanently *gone* (RB-002 §2.8). The instrument above shows those two modeled regimes side by side. A covered outdoor kitchen sits between them — and gets the worst trade available: the roof blocks the vertical escape that made dilution work, without supplying the engineered exhaust path, makeup air, or room turnover that make indoor kitchens survivable. The RB papers model the two endpoints, not the in-between; what follows is what can honestly be inferred from them.

## Under a roof, the hood's misses stop being free

Outdoors, first-pass capture is the only capture, and RB-002's consolation is that the escaped fraction disperses irreversibly into the sky (§2.8). Under a roof that consolation is gone. RB-011's transport analysis has escaped grease aerosol traveling until it deposits on the first surface it encounters, and notes that open-sky sites have no ceiling to intercept it (§1) — a covered kitchen has exactly such a ceiling, so the roof underside and everything beneath it become the deposition surfaces. Grease films on structure are the fuel in the flame-contact fire scenario RB-011 documents (§3.6.1). Smoke that would have cleared in seconds hangs at head height. The hood's capture percentage — a comfort metric in the open — becomes the number that decides what your ceiling accumulates.

## Shelter does not shrink the requirement

It is tempting to reason that a protected, low-wind site needs less hood. The sizing physics says: less than a windy site, but never little. Even RB-008's most favorable exposure class — sheltered, wind under 3 mph — still requires roughly three times the bare plume flow (K_CFM = 3.0, RB-008 §2.2), because the hood must swallow the plume plus the ambient air it entrains regardless of weather. For a 60,000 BTU grill under a 48-inch wall hood that is 1,200 CFM minimum whatever the site (see [what CFM you need](/questions/what-cfm-do-i-need/)). The wind questions and sizing details for covered installs are in [Can I use an outdoor range hood under a covered patio?](/questions/hood-under-covered-patio/).

## The more enclosed, the less this site can answer

A pergola with open lattice, a solid-roof patio open on three sides, a screened summer kitchen, a pool house with a grill wall — these sit at different points on a spectrum the research does not quantify. The honest guidance scales anyway: the closer your kitchen is to being a room, the more it needs a real exhaust system sized for capture, and the more the decision belongs to your appliance manual and local code authority rather than to a physics site. The safety mechanisms — combustion gases, fire clearances, what a hood can and cannot promise under a roof — are covered in [Is it safe to grill under a covered patio?](/questions/is-it-safe-to-grill-under-a-covered-patio/).
